Understanding Customers

A useful business idea becomes stronger when you understand the people you want to serve. Customers are not just numbers; they are people with specific needs, situations, preferences, constraints, and expectations.

Learning path progress 3 of 6

Know who you are serving

One of the first questions a business should answer is: Who is this for?

A product or service rarely needs to be designed for absolutely everyone. A clearer understanding of the people you want to serve can help you communicate better, build more useful offers, and make better business decisions.

Instead of saying: “Everyone is my customer,” ask: “Which specific people have this problem?”

What should you learn about customers?

  • What problem are they trying to solve?
  • How often does the problem occur?
  • What matters most to them?
  • What alternatives are they using now?
  • What makes an existing solution difficult or inconvenient?
  • What price or cost considerations affect their decision?

Listen before you assume

Business owners can easily make assumptions about what customers want. Research and conversation can reveal something different.

Asking people questions, observing how they work, studying existing alternatives, and testing small ideas can provide useful evidence before investing heavily in a product or service.

Customer feedback does not mean every request must be followed. It means the business has information that can help it decide what to improve, test, keep, or change.

A simple customer profile

You can begin with a simple description of the person you want to help: their situation, their problem, what they currently do, and what a useful outcome would look like.
